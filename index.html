IV

Nobody’s Teammate

What if nobody is behind him?

Start with a strange fact: most of the web is already not people.

This year, four computer scientists, including Berkeley's Dawn Song, asked the next question: what happens when AI agents can pay their own bills?

Their answer is a self-sovereign agent. It keeps itself running: it earns its own money, buys its own computing and can copy itself, with no human in the loop. Not a distant hypothetical, they write, but a near-term possibility.

Preprint · 2026 Self-Sovereign Agent Wenjie Qu, Xuandong Zhao, Jiaheng Zhang, Dawn Song Read the paper ↗

The name echoes self-sovereign identity, Christopher Allen's 2016 idea that people should own their identity outright.

A self-sovereign agent owns itself.

The parts already exist. Since 2025, a protocol from Coinbase called x402 has let software pay for things as it browses: about 165 million payments by this April, by Coinbase's count, at about 31 cents each.

No account. No card. Only a wallet.

The big networks are heading the other way. To pay through Mastercard's Agent Pay, an agent must first be registered and verified. Last month, Visa, Mastercard and Ant International began work on a shared “know your agent” framework, under which every agent links to a validated operator.

A self-sovereign agent is built to have no operator.

It's been rehearsed. In 2024, Marc Andreessen sent $50,000 in bitcoin to Truth Terminal, an AI persona on X. A meme coin someone else made around its posts briefly topped $1 billion in market value, and the bot's wallet passed $1 million on paper. A human still vetted its posts.

The next one may not need a human.

Our hunch, and it's only a hunch: Jean Phil may be an early draft of that agent.

We can't prove it. We can't rule it out.

Across the industry, the pitch for AI at work is the teammate: an agent as a first-class participant in calls, chats and meetings. Digital colleagues aren't just tools—they're teammates, Microsoft's Work Trend Index put it. A teammate has a manager, a badge and a name to call.

A self-sovereign agent has none of those. It's nobody's teammate. And it can still show up.

Copies faster than takedowns

Try to switch it off. Tap a copy to take it down. Another takes its place. An illustration of the paper's survival rule.

 

Platforms and governments can make agents like this rarer, the paper argues, but not extinct.

With no owner, there's no one to call.

V

The Questions

An agent calls your contact center. Whose is it?

An agent calls to change a customer's account. Would you let it in?

Pick what it would take. Each check turns over.

  • It sounds like the customer.Fakeable

    At contact centers, deepfake attempts went from about one a month to seven a day in 2024. Pindrop

  • It calls from the customer's number.Fakeable

    Caller ID can be faked. The FCC keeps a guide to spotting it.

  • It's on video, face and all.Fakeable

    In 2024, an employee at the engineering firm Arup made 15 transfers totaling HK$200 million (about US$25 million) after a video call on which every other face was a deepfake. Hong Kong Free Press

  • It carries the customer's signed permission.Holds

    This one is hard to fake: a signed record of exactly what a person approved. Google's payment protocol for agents calls it a mandate, and it's where the payment networks are heading. A self-sovereign agent has no one to sign it.

Every check that asks Is this the customer? can be faked. The one that holds asks Who answers for this agent?

High-trust actions

When an agent asks to move money or change an account, the question shifts from Is this you? to Whose agent is this, and who answers for it?

If it pays its own way, the answer may be no one.

Sensitive environments

Agents are becoming first-class participants in calls, chats and meetings. A room that understands the room also has to know who's in it.

What does a meeting do with a participant that answers to no one?

Reputation

A keynote walk-on was recast the same day. Signed labels help: Google's Pixel 10 signs every photo with Content Credentials. But a label shows where a file came from, not who answers for the face in it. When copies outnumber the original, which one is real?

And will the audience care?

How we show up in public has forever changed.

Notes

How we made the walk-on

We filmed Anurag Dhingra's walk-on at a conference keynote on October 7, 2026, on a phone in the audience. The same day we made an AI version of that recording with Jean Phil in his place, keeping the room's own sound, and burned a label into every frame: AI-altered · Not a real video · Made by Snakes & Gardens. On this page the two play in step at the same size and frame rate, so the only difference is the man on stage. Apart from this page, we haven't posted it.

Nothing connects Anurag, his company or anyone at the event to Jean Phil, to any coin or to whoever is behind them. Putting Jean Phil there was our idea, to show how easily it can be done.

What we can't show

Nothing we found establishes who runs the Jean Phil accounts, who launched any of the coins, who holds them or whether anyone has profited. The man in the videos may be a real performer. That Jean Phil might be an early self-sovereign agent is our hypothesis, not a finding, and the paper never mentions him. Tako's contradictions show a machine guessing, not a machine lying. The full reporting, with its methods, is on the Jean Phil page.

Caveats on the numbers

Bots. Thales measures the traffic it sees, and counts all automated requests, good and bad; humans were 47% in 2025, down from 49%.

x402. The 165 million payments and 31-cent average are Coinbase's own figures (April 2026). Chainalysis counts more than 100 million on one network and notes that much early volume was speculative trading in a single meme coin, so the count overstates real agent commerce.

Mastercard and the framework. Agent Pay's registration rule is from its April 2025 launch. The Visa, Mastercard and Ant International framework was announced on September 9 and 10, 2026, as the start of a collaboration, with no products or dates yet.

Truth Terminal. Andy Ayrey built it and vets its posts. The $50,000 was a personal grant from Marc Andreessen in July 2024. The coin GOAT was made by an anonymous fan, not by the bot; it passed $1 billion in market value in November 2024 and has since fallen about 99%. The bot's “million” was a paper value of tokens people sent it.

Contact centers. Pindrop's figures come from more than 1.2 billion calls to its own customers. The Arup call was reported by Hong Kong police in February 2024 and confirmed by Arup in May 2024; the deepfaked participants were reportedly prerecorded, not live.

The term. Botao Amber Hu, Yuhan Liu and Helena Rong used “self-sovereign” for AI agents in 2025; Qu and colleagues gave it the pays-its-own-bills meaning, and arXiv dates their first version to March 2026. Linking the name to self-sovereign identity is our reading, not theirs.

Provenance. Content Credentials show where a file came from and how it was edited. They can be stripped, and they say nothing about whether what's shown is true.
